# Respect the frame block, keep the feed

**Short answer: yes, an individual in Norway can legally publish a vertical-scroll feed of daily web games, provided it only frames games that permit framing (or whose owners have agreed) and falls back to "open in a new tab" for the rest. Stripping X-Frame-Options/CSP headers so that blocked games load, and handing users an extension that does it, is the one part of the plan that turns a low-risk hobby project into a high-risk one.** EU copyright law, which Norway follows through the EEA Agreement, treats framing freely available content as lawful. The EU Court of Justice has also held that framing which *circumvents* a rightholder's anti-framing measures is a new "communication to the public" that needs permission, and the header-stripping extension does exactly that ([CURIA, C-392/19](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/p1_3453439)). Several other problems point the same way: the risk of building a distribution tool for circumvention, NYT's record of enforcing Wordle, security exposure for users, Chrome Web Store policy, Google ad policy against framed content, and Norway's strict cookie-consent rules since 2025. The practical recommendation follows. Keep the scroll feed. Make every card work as a plain link-out. Embed in iframes only those games that load without circumvention or whose developer has said yes in writing. Never embed NYT Wordle. Keep the header-stripping extension as a private, localhost-only tool, which is how it is built today. Hold off on ads until permissions are in place.

> **Not legal advice.** This report is written for a non-lawyer hobbyist and summarises public sources; it is not a substitute for advice from a Norwegian lawyer. **Important limitation on sources:** in this research session, the primary pages could not be fetched at all. That includes the CJEU judgments on curia.europa.eu, Norwegian statutes on lovdata.no, NYT's terms of service, every game site's own terms page, Google's policy pages and the OWASP/Chrome developer docs. Most findings below therefore rest on **search-engine snippets and secondary write-ups (law-firm briefings, news reports, blogs)**, not on reading the full original texts. Nobody tested which of the eleven games actually send anti-framing headers. Treat specific quotations and section numbers as leads to verify, not as checked facts.

## Framing is lawful in Europe until you defeat a block

The EU rules on linking and framing come from four Court of Justice cases, and Norwegian courts treat them as highly persuasive even though Norway is outside the EU. In *Svensson* (2014) the Court held that linking to works freely accessible on another site is not a new communication to the public, because those works "were freely accessible to anyone with an internet connection" ([Columbia Global Freedom of Expression](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/svensson-v-sverige-ab)). In *BestWater* (2014) it extended this to **framing**: embedding content that is already freely and lawfully available elsewhere reaches no new public and uses no new technical means, so it needs no permission ([Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/bestwater-cjeu-embeds-decision-on-framed-content-in-order/)). *GS Media* (2016) added a twist that matters only if you link to pirated copies. A for-profit linker is presumed to know when the target was posted without consent ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2016/09/the-cjeus-decision-in-igs-mediai-connecting-the-dots-on-hyperlinking)). That would be relevant if the feed framed an infringing Wordle clone, but not when it frames the official sites.

The decisive case is ***VG Bild-Kunst* (C-392/19, Grand Chamber, 9 March 2021)**. The Court held that framing "is a communication to the public where it circumvents protection measures against framing adopted or imposed by the copyright holder" ([CURIA](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/p1_3453439)). It also held that a rightholder can limit consent to framing **only through effective technological measures**, not through notices or terms. The reasoning is that ordinary users cannot otherwise tell whether framing is opposed ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2021/03/vg-bild-kunst-the-saga-on-legality-of-linking-continues); [Latham & Watkins](https://www.latham.london/2021/04/vg-bild-kunst-practical-implications-of-the-cjeus-latest-decision-on-linking-and-framing/)). In the case itself, the collecting society named the "X-frame option" as one of two simple anti-framing measures. That was a party's argument, not a holding of the Court ([CMS case tracker](https://cms.law/en/bel/publication/artificial-intelligence-and-copyright-case-tracker/germany-ddb-v-v.-bild-kunst)). The parties settled in March 2024, so no national court ever applied the test to the facts ([CMS case tracker](https://cms.law/en/bel/publication/artificial-intelligence-and-copyright-case-tracker/germany-ddb-v-v.-bild-kunst)).

**No court anywhere has yet decided whether an X-Frame-Options or CSP `frame-ancestors` header counts as an "effective" anti-framing measure.** The honest position is "very probably yes, but untested." The header is the standard, browser-enforced way a site says "do not frame me." Every major browser honours it by default, and a user can only get past it by deliberately installing a tool built for that purpose. There is a fair counter-argument: many sites send these headers for security reasons or because their hosting framework adds them automatically, not because they made a copyright decision. But the Court chose an objective technical test precisely so that nobody has to guess intent. A hobbyist should not plan around winning that argument. One more point matters here. The whole web game page, meaning its code, graphics, text and puzzles, contains protected expression even though the underlying game idea does not. Copyright does not protect game mechanics ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2022/01/12/world-handled-wordle-ripoff-just-fine-without-any-ip-action/)), but an iframe shows the entire page, so the framing analysis applies in full.

### Norway follows the same line, and adds facilitator liability

Norway implements the EU InfoSoc Directive through the EEA Agreement. The 2018 Copyright Act (*åndsverkloven*) gives authors the exclusive right to make works available to the public. **§ 99 forbids circumventing "effective technical protection systems"** used to control copying or making available to the public ([Lovdata, via search snippet](https://lovdata.no/nav/lov/2018-06-15-40/kap7/%C2%A7101); [Stortinget lovvedtak](https://www.stortinget.no/no/Saker-og-publikasjoner/Vedtak/Beslutninger/Lovvedtak/2017-2018/vedtak-201718-053/)). No Norwegian court decision on framing, on *VG Bild-Kunst*, or on header circumvention was found. The closest precedent is the Supreme Court's ***Napster.no* (Rt. 2005 s. 41)**. There the court held a linking site liable for **contributing (*medvirkning*)** to others' unlawful making-available, without needing to decide whether linking itself was an infringing act ([Jusnytt](https://www.jusnytt.no/en/de-siste-nyhetene/1-napster-ansvar-for-tilgjengeliggjoring)). For this project, that means the argument "the circumvention happens in the user's browser, not on my site" is weak. If you build a public feed that only works once visitors install your header-stripping extension, Norwegian courts already have a familiar route to holding you responsible.

### Distributing the extension is a separate, sharper risk

EU law has a second hook besides the communication right. InfoSoc Article 6(2), mirrored in Norwegian law, bans distributing devices "primarily designed" to circumvent effective technological measures ([SCL on *Nintendo*](https://www.scl.org/3002-ecj-judgment-on-circumvention-of-games-console-protection/)). In *Nintendo v PC Box* (2014) the Court read "effective technological measure" broadly, while requiring proportionality and attention to a device's other legitimate uses ([IPKat](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2014/01/breaking-news-cjeu-says-that.html)). Advocate General Szpunar argued in 2020 that anti-framing measures control only "a manner of displaying works" and so fall outside Article 6, but the Court did not adopt his overall approach ([CMS case tracker](https://cms.law/en/bel/publication/artificial-intelligence-and-copyright-case-tracker/germany-ddb-v-v.-bild-kunst)). The result is unsettled law with a clear direction of travel. A general developer tool that strips headers for testing has a decent proportionality defence. An extension built specifically to make NYT Wordle and ten named games load inside one particular feed does not. Generic tools of this kind exist and market themselves as letting "all sites be loaded in iframes, despite X-Frame-Options header settings" ([GitHub: Ignore X-Frame-Options](https://github.com/ThomazPom/Moz-Ext-Ignore-X-Frame-Options)). Their existence shows the technique is easy, not that it is lawful for this use. End users who install such an extension technically carry out the circumvention themselves, but enforcement against individuals is improbable. The exposure sits with whoever runs the feed and ships the tool.

## The US is split, but it reaches you anyway

US law has no rule tied to anti-framing measures, and the courts disagree about embedding in general. Under the Ninth Circuit's **"server test"**, a site that embeds content hosted on someone else's server does not "display" it and is not a direct infringer. That court reaffirmed the test in *Hunley v. Instagram* (2023) ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/22-15293/22-15293-2023-07-17.html)), and the **Supreme Court declined to review it in 2025** in *McGucken v. Valnet* ([Nixon Peabody](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2025/07/09/supreme-court-declines-to-resolve-looming-split-on-embedded-content-rights)). Federal judges in New York have rejected the test in *Goldman v. Breitbart* (2018), *Nicklen v. Sinclair* (2021) and *McGucken v. Newsweek* (2022), holding that embedding can infringe the display right ([Foley & Lardner](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2018/03/case-to-watch-goldman-v-breitbart-involving-embedd/); [Weil](https://www.weil.com/-/media/mailings/2021/q2/ip-media-alert-august-9-2021embedding-the-server-test-rift-sdny-decision-bucks-ninth-circuit-once-ag.pdf); [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/publications/2022/03/another-blow-to-ninth-circuits-server-test)). In April 2026 the Second Circuit itself avoided the question and decided a case on licence grounds instead ([Proskauer, via search summary](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/second-circuit-sidesteps-server-test-in-embedded-video-copyright-ruling-highlighting-how-platform-terms-matter)). NYT is based in New York.

This matters to a Norwegian hobbyist for practical rather than doctrinal reasons. US rightholders can send DMCA notices to US-based hosts, CDNs, GitHub and extension stores wherever the operator lives. NYT has done exactly that at scale, with notices covering roughly 1,900 GitHub forks of a Wordle clone in March 2024 ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/puzzle/nearly-2000-wordle-clones-targeted-as-nyt-issues-dmca-takedown/)). If you deliberately strip a site's anti-framing headers, you also lose any argument that the owner impliedly allowed embedding. You may invite a claim under the US anti-circumvention law (DMCA § 1201), though none has been tested. Whether US courts would take jurisdiction over a Norwegian site was not researched.

## NYT enforces hard, and contract and trademark law fill the gaps

**NYT's terms limit its content to "personal, non-commercial use"** and ban tools "designed to data mine or scrape" its content. They warn of sanctions against users "and those assisting the user" ([Hypebeast](https://hypebeast.com/2023/8/new-york-times-prohibits-ai-scaping-content-training-info); [CDP Institute](https://www.cdpinstitute.org/news/nyt-acts-to-protect-against-content-scraping/)). These quotations come from news coverage of the August 2023 update. The current terms themselves could not be read, and no clause that expressly mentions "framing" was confirmed. No official Wordle embed widget or licensing programme was found. There is a legal subtlety here. Under *VG Bild-Kunst*, terms alone cannot withdraw copyright consent to framing. And a website's terms often bind a visitor who never clicked "I agree" only weakly. On remand in *Ryanair v PR Aviation*, a Dutch appeal court reportedly held that Ryanair's browse-wrap terms did not stop a scraper ([Mediareport.nl](https://mediareport.nl/auteursrecht/07032018/ryanair-kan-scrapen-niet-met-browse-wrap-verbieden/)). That defence weakens quickly once you have built the integration knowingly or received a cease-and-desist letter.

NYT's enforcement record shows what it cares about. In 2022 it forced the Wordle Archive replay site offline ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/wordle-archive-shuts-down-after-new-york-times-issues-legal-demand/1100-6501588/)). In 2024 its DMCA notices claimed the Wordle name, the green-and-yellow tiles and the 5×6 grid, although critics called that overreach ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2024/03/13/1238142507/cease-desist-new-york-times-wordle-spin-offs); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/03/13/new-york-times-wordle-clones-copyright-knock-offs/)). **On 4 November 2026 the US trademark board sustained NYT's opposition to registering WORLDLE**, finding it likely to be confused with WORDLE ([TTAB opinion](https://business.cch.com/ipld/NewYorkTimesCompanyWorldleLLCTTAB20261104110626.pdf); [Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/2547389/nyt-blocks-worldle-tm-bid-over-confusion-with-wordle-)). That decision blocked registration only, not the game's continued use ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2024/06/06/the-ny-times-challenges-worldles-name-as-the-war-on-wordle-likes-continues/)). The record names the applicant as Worldle LLC, while CBC named developer Kory McDonald ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/radio/asithappens/worldle-versus-wordle-1.7224655)). **It is not confirmed whether that is the same Worldle as Teuteuf's worldle.teuteuf.fr in your feed.** In Europe NYT's trademark position is patchier. It lost against a German "Wordle" in 2024 ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2024/07/21/new-york-times-unable-to-push-germanys-wordle-out-of-puzzle-market)) but won a UK invalidation in 2026 ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/oh-my-wordle-new-york-times-succeeds-invalidating-uk-wordle-trade-mark-without-uk)). No NYT action against a site that merely *links to or frames* the real Wordle was found. Every documented target copied the game or borrowed the name. Even so, an ad-supported feed that shows "Wordle" next to other "-dle" games touches exactly the brand interests NYT litigates over. wordle.global carries the Wordle name in its domain, which makes featuring it slightly riskier. A GitHub project called "Wordle Global" uses a PolyForm Noncommercial licence ([GitHub Hugo0/wordle](https://github.com/Hugo0/wordle/)), but it was not confirmed that this is the same site.

In Norway the backstop is the Marketing Control Act. **§ 25 (good business practice between businesses) and § 30 (imitation)** supplement trademark law. In two December 2021 judgments the Supreme Court treated them as residual rules with a high threshold, finding no breach where competitors' names were bought as search keywords ([HR-2021-2479-A](https://lovdata.no/dokument/HRSIV/avgjorelse/hr-2021-2479-a); [HR-2021-2480-A](https://www.domstol.no/globalassets/upload/hret/avgjorelser/2021/desember-2021/hr-2021-2480-a.pdf)). Simply naming a game to identify it is therefore defensible. A § 25 claim becomes more plausible when the overall business model systematically appropriates other people's games for your own ad revenue, presents them misleadingly, or defeats their technical barriers. The safe habits are plain-text names, no borrowed logos in your own branding, a visible "not affiliated with" notice, and a link to each official site.

The smaller games are mostly one-person or small-studio businesses funded by advertising. Teuteuf Games runs Worldle and Flagle ([flagle.io/about](https://www.flagle.io/about)). Abe Train's Trainwreck Labs runs Globle, which Forbes describes as "funded primarily by advertising" ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/ianshepherd/2026/11/05/globle-shows-how-free-games-can-become-serious-businesses-for-creators/)). Magnitudle and Krillion are solo projects ([magnitudle.com](https://magnitudle.com/); [krillionwiki.com](https://krillionwiki.com/basics/who-made-krillion)). Ownership of Plain Game, Daily Roll, wordle.global, WhoWhatWhere and Daily Unfold was not found. None of their terms pages could be read. A document circulating as Teuteuf's confidential "Distribution Integration Guide" says partners receive the iframe snippet only "once a partnership agreement has been signed" ([Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/811311925/Integration-Guide)). Its authenticity is unverified, but it suggests Teuteuf already has a permission route. Developer reactions to uninvited framing range widely. Some on itch.io say they are happy "as long as my game is played" ([itch.io](https://itch.io/post/1066888)). The Sqword developer, after finding aggregators framing his free game with ads, made it display a shock image whenever it was framed ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/sqword-game-dev-sneaks-goatse-onto-a-dozen-sites-that-stole-his-game/)). What decides the reaction is money made on top of their work without asking.

## The extension weakens user security and fits a known malware pattern

X-Frame-Options and CSP `frame-ancestors` exist to stop **clickjacking**, where a hostile page loads a real site invisibly or deceptively in a frame and tricks a logged-in user into clicking ([OWASP cheat sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Clickjacking_Defense_Cheat_Sheet.html); [Chrome Lighthouse](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/lighthouse/best-practices/clickjacking-mitigation?hl=en)). The current extension in this repo removes the **entire** `x-frame-options` and `content-security-policy` headers for all eleven domains, including every `*.nytimes.com` subdomain. Login and account pages are therefore covered too. Removing the whole CSP also switches off the game pages' defences against script injection, not just their frame rule. Today the rule fires only when the iframe sits on `localhost`/`127.0.0.1`, which limits the blast radius to your own machine. A public version would have to trust your public domain instead. Anyone who later controls that domain would then inherit the power to frame NYT and the other sites unprotected, in every browser that still has the extension installed. That could happen through a cross-site-scripting bug, a compromised hosting account or dependency, a sale of the domain, or the domain expiring and being re-registered. Users rarely uninstall "set and forget" helpers, so this exposure outlives the project.

That pattern is familiar to store reviewers for a reason. In a 2024–25 campaign, **16 malicious Chrome extensions with about 3.2 million users** used the same `declarativeNetRequest` `modifyHeaders` technique to blank CSP headers and inject scripts. GitLab Threat Intelligence reported them, Google removed them, and GitLab described degrading CSP without informed consent as a clear breach of Chrome Web Store policy ([GitLab Security Tech Note](https://gitlab-com.gitlab.io/gl-security/security-tech-notes/threat-intelligence-tech-notes/malicious-browser-extensions-feb-2025/); [Techzine](https://www.techzine.eu/news/security/129236/malicious-extensions-in-chrome-web-store-affected-3-2-million-users)). Neither Chrome nor Edge bans header-modifying extensions outright, and several "ignore X-Frame" tools are live in the Chrome Web Store. The best known of them warns that it should be used only temporarily, for development and testing, because it disables important security mechanisms ([Chrome Web Store listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/ignore-x-frame-headers/gleekbfjekiniecknbkamfmkohkpodhe)). Both stores do require least privilege. Chrome says to "request access to the narrowest permissions necessary" ([Chrome Program Policies](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/webstore/program-policies/policies?hl=en)). Edge says an extension "must not jeopardize or compromise user security" ([Edge developer policies](https://learn.microsoft.com/legal/microsoft-edge/extensions/developer-policies)). A consumer extension that weakens NYT's security for the benefit of one third-party site looks very different to a reviewer than a user-controlled developer tool. It also gives framed brands an obvious target for complaints to the store. No documented case was found of an X-Frame-stripping extension being rejected specifically for that behaviour, so treat rejection or later takedown as a real risk rather than a certainty.

Browsers will also undercut the feed regardless of the extension. Chrome kept third-party cookies switched on ([usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/what-is-google-privacy-sandbox/)). But since Chrome 115 it has **partitioned storage** such as localStorage in third-party frames ([Chrome: Storage Partitioning](https://privacysandbox.google.com/cookies/storage-partitioning)). Safari blocks third-party cookies by default, and Firefox partitions them ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Privacy/Guides/third-party_cookies)). In practice, a player's Wordle or Globle streak inside your feed will be separate from their streak on the real site. Paid NYT logins will often fail inside the frame, and Safari and Firefox users will see blank frames for any blocked game because the extension is Chromium-only.

## Norwegian cookie law and ad policy make a public feed heavier than it looks

**Since 1 January 2025, the new Electronic Communications Act (*ekomloven*) § 3-15 requires GDPR-standard consent for non-essential cookies and similar technologies.** Consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous ([Regjeringen](https://www.regjeringen.no/no/aktuelt/endringer-i-lover-og-forskrifter-fra-1.-januar-2025-fra-digitaliserings-og-forvaltningsdepartementet/id3079637/); [CMS Norway](https://cms.law/en/nor/publication/ny-ekomlovgivning)). Datatilsynet supervises the consent requirement ([delegation regulation](https://regjeringen.no/contentassets/0111de0d5d524296aae662e35869f898/forskrift-om-delegering-av-myndighet-etter-lov-13.-desember-2024-nr.-76-om-elektronisk-kommunikasjon-ekomloven-.pdf)). Its April 2025 guidance reportedly says rejecting must take no more effort than accepting, and that embedded third-party content should be checked for what it sends on page load ([Cookie Information](https://cookieinformation.com/blog/norwegian-cookie-guidelines-explained/)). Secondary sources disagree on whether browser settings can still count as consent ([Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/e-com-act-regulatory-updates-for-cookie-use-in-norway/) versus [Cookie Information](https://cookieinformation.com/blog/norwegian-cookie-guidelines-explained/)). Datatilsynet is enforcing. In June 2025 it issued a fine and five reprimands over tracking pixels ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/the-norwegian-sa-issues-one-administrative-fine-and-five-reprimands-for-unlawful-sharing-of_fi)).

The key case for an iframe feed is ***Fashion ID* (C-40/17, 2019)**. A site that embeds third-party content which sends visitors' data to the third party on page load is a **joint controller** for that collection and transmission, and must obtain prior consent and inform visitors ([CJEU press release](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/p1_2278332/ro/); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/cjeu-ruling-on-websites-using-facebook-like-buttons)). A feed that automatically loads ten or more game sites as the visitor scrolls, each with its own analytics and ad tags, fits that description. You would need click-to-load placeholders or a consent gate before each frame loads, plus a privacy notice naming every embedded provider. A feed of link-out cards that stores "done today" only in the visitor's own browser avoids nearly all of this. A purely personal localhost tool sits under the GDPR household exemption.

Monetising with Google ads is close to a non-starter for a framing feed. Google's publisher policies forbid ads on screens without publisher content. They define publisher content as material "created or managed by the publisher" ([Google publisher policies](https://support.google.com/publisherpolicies/answer/11112688)), and they specifically name "mirroring, framing, scraping or rewriting of content from other sources without adding value." Framing is described as displaying someone else's site "without permission from the owners" ([Google replicated-content policy](https://support.google.com/publisherpolicies/answer/11190248?hl=en)). A site that needs an extension to defeat the owner's refusal is about as clear a case of "without permission" as exists. A separate risk is that Google's Safe Browsing may flag a site that appears to operate on behalf of another brand "without making the relationship clear" as social engineering ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/advanced/security/social-engineering)). A "Deceptive site ahead" warning would block the whole feed. NYT login screens rendered inside a non-NYT domain are exactly the pattern phishing training warns users about. No case of a game aggregator being flagged this way was found.

## Every legitimate hub links out or signs a deal

The existing daily-game hubs show the safe models. The open-source **"dles" list catalogues 544 daily web games as plain outbound links** ([GitHub qubit10/dles](https://github.com/qubit10/dles)). Hubdle, Dle Hunt and Dailies.games pitch themselves as hubs, though whether they embed could not be checked ([Product Hunt: Hubdle](https://www.producthunt.com/products/hubdle/launches)). The businesses that do show games inside their own pages all have agreements. Puzzmo licenses a branded version to publishers on "rev-shares on both subscription and advertising revenue" ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2023/10/20/puzzmo-hearst-zach-gage-new-york-times)). Arkadium licenses embeddable games to publishers ([Arkadium](https://corporate.arkadium.com/game-licensing/)). Portals such as CrazyGames require developer submission and their SDK ([CrazyGames docs](https://docs.crazygames.com/faq/)), and GameDistribution supplies an official embed snippet ([GitHub gd-embed-game](https://github.com/GameDistribution/gd-embed-game)). CrazyGames even documents CSP `frame-ancestors` as the way developers block unwanted framing ([CrazyGames sitelock](https://docs.crazygames.com/resources/html5/sitelock)). That is the same header your extension removes. No reputable hub was found framing third-party daily games without agreement.

The vertical-scroll experience survives without framing. Each card can show the game's name, creator, your own short description and a large "Play" button. The button opens the real site in a new tab, or on Android in a Custom Tab, which Google recommends over WebViews for third-party content ([Android developers](https://developer.android.com/guide/webapps)). A local "done today" tick that the user taps keeps the routine feel. Games that load in an iframe without help, or whose developers say yes, can play inline, with the link-out as a fallback in case they add a frame block later. Solo developers such as Teej (Magnitudle), kp (Krillion) and Abe Train (Globle) are reachable, and a short email asking for permission is realistic. It should describe exactly how the game will appear, whether ads sit near it, and offer attribution and removal on request. Over time you could add a few games of your own, built from permissively licensed code such as the MIT-licensed guessle or the CC0 MiniWordle ([GitHub jakerella/guessle](https://github.com/jakerella/guessle); [GitHub xem/MiniWordle](https://github.com/xem/MiniWordle)). These would carry original names and designs and no Wordle look-alike styling. A Norwegian-language word game would also give the feed something no link list has.

## Risk assessment and recommended path

The table rates each variant for a Norway-based individual. "Low" means ordinary good-practice steps make problems unlikely. "Medium" means a realistic chance of complaints, takedowns or policy strikes, but limited legal exposure if you respond promptly. "High" means conduct that current case law points against, aimed at an owner known to enforce.

| Variant | Copyright (EU/NO) | Contract / trademark / unfair competition | Platform & security | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Private feed on localhost with the extension, for your own use only (the current repo setup) | Theoretical § 99 issue, no public communication | Negligible | Risk limited to your own machine | **Low** |
| Public feed of link-out cards (name, own text, link, local "done" tick) | Lawful linking under *Svensson* | Low with plain names and a no-affiliation notice | Privacy notice; minimal cookies | **Low** |
| Public feed that frames only games which load unaided, no ads, attribution, click-to-load | Lawful under *BestWater* | Owner objections possible; drop on request | Cookie consent needed (*Fashion ID*); streaks don't sync | **Low–medium** |
| Same as above, with permission from each framed developer | No issue | No issue | Consent still needed | **Low** |
| Unaided framing plus Google ads around the frames | Still lawful copyright-wise | Higher § 25 and developer-objection risk | Breaches AdSense framing / replicated-content policy | **Medium** |
| Public feed that frames NYT Wordle in any form | Lawful only if it loads unaided, which is unverified | NYT terms ("personal, non-commercial"), aggressive enforcement, trademark | Login and phishing look-alike issues | **Medium–high** |
| Public feed that relies on users installing your header-stripping extension (GitHub or private download) | Communication to a new public (*VG Bild-Kunst*); facilitator liability (*Napster.no*); device-distribution ban | Defeats any implied-licence argument; strengthens unfair-competition claims | Exposes users to clickjacking and lost CSP | **High** |
| Same extension published in the Chrome Web Store or Edge Add-ons | As above, plus a public record of the purpose | Brand complaints to the store | Matches a known malware pattern; rejection or takedown likely | **High** |
| Server-side proxy that fetches pages and strips headers | Copies on your server; loses even the US server test | Hits NYT's anti-scraping clause | Your origin becomes a phishing-style host | **High** |

The recommended path has four stages. First, keep the extension exactly as it is, a localhost-only personal tool, and do not publish it or tell site visitors to install it. Second, launch the public site as a scroll feed of link-out cards, with plain-text game names, creator credits, a "not affiliated with" line, a contact and removal address, a short privacy notice, and no third-party trackers. Third, test each game's headers. Where a game loads in an iframe unaided, offer it inline behind a tap-to-load placeholder, keep the new-tab fallback, and email its developer for written permission. If a developer says no, or objects later, switch that game back to link-out within a day or two. Treat NYT Wordle as link-out only, permanently. Fourth, add ads only once the inline games are permitted and the site has enough original content (your own descriptions, your own games) to count as publisher content. Pair the ads with a proper consent banner that makes rejecting as easy as accepting. Before monetising or registering as an *enkeltpersonforetak*, have the specific claims here checked against the primary texts on curia.europa.eu, lovdata.no and the game sites' terms, ideally with a short consultation with a Norwegian IP lawyer.

## Conclusion

The legal line in Europe is unusually clear for an internet question, and it falls in a convenient place for this project. The owner's own technical setting decides whether framing is permitted. If a site lets itself be framed, you may frame it. If it blocks framing, defeating the block is the infringing act, and building a tool to defeat it makes you the obvious defendant. The header-stripping extension is not a minor implementation detail; it decides whether the feed is lawful. It also triggers almost every other risk at once: store review, user security, ad policy and NYT's attention.

The more useful insight is that the extension buys surprisingly little. Storage partitioning already splits players' streaks between the feed and the real site, Safari and Firefox users cannot run a Chrome extension, and paid logins break inside frames. The real product, a calm daily routine that walks you through your games one swipe at a time, survives almost intact with link-out cards and a handful of permitted embeds. Getting each developer's permission is mainly a matter of sending an email, and it turns a legal grey area into a feature you can advertise.
