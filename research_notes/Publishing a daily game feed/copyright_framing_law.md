## 1. CJEU case law: Svensson, BestWater, GS Media, VG Bild-Kunst. What did VG Bild-Kunst hold, and does it cover X-Frame-Options/CSP?

### Takeaway
Under EU law, framing content that the rightholder has made freely available online is **not** a new "communication to the public" (Svensson 2014, BestWater 2014). That changes when the rightholder has used **effective technological measures against framing**. In that case, framing that circumvents them reaches a "new public" and needs the rightholder's authorisation (VG Bild-Kunst, Grand Chamber, 9 March 2021). The rightholder can limit consent **only** through effective technical measures, not through notices or terms. VG Bild-Kunst itself pointed to the X-Frame-Options header as an example of such a measure. The Court never ruled on whether XFO or CSP `frame-ancestors` counts as "effective", and no case found has decided that question. Still, XFO/CSP is the standard browser-level anti-framing mechanism, and stripping it is hard to describe as anything other than circumventing a measure against framing.

### Cited Findings
- **Svensson (C-466/12, 13 Feb 2014):** a website owner may, without authorisation, redirect users through hyperlinks to protected works that are freely accessible on another site. There is no new public because the works "were freely accessible to anyone with an internet connection". — [Columbia Global Freedom of Expression, Svensson](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/svensson-v-sverige-ab); [CMS, "CJEU rules that hyperlinks are generally lawful"](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/cjeu-rules-that-hyperlinks-are-generally-lawful); [Kluwer Copyright Blog, "it is allowed to link, at least in principle"](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-court-of-justice-on-links-it-is-allowed-to-link-at-least-in-principle/)
- Commentary on Svensson notes that a link can later become unlawful if the target site stops being freely available (for example, it moves behind restrictions) while the link still works. — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/cjeu-rules-that-hyperlinks-are-generally-lawful)
- **BestWater (C-348/13, reasoned order, 21 Oct 2014):** embedding another person's content in a website through framing, where the content was already freely and lawfully available on a third-party site, is not a communication to the public. The content is neither communicated to a new public nor communicated by a different technical means. — [Stanford WILMAP, BestWater](https://wilmap.stanford.edu/node/29784); [Kluwer Copyright Blog, "BestWater: CJEU embeds decision on framed content in order"](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/bestwater-cjeu-embeds-decision-on-framed-content-in-order/); [Heuking, "ECJ: framing alone does not constitute the public communication of a work"](https://www.heuking.de/en/news-events/articles/ecj-framing-alone-does-not-constitute-the-public-communication-of-a-work.html)
- **GS Media (C-160/15, 8 Sept 2016):** linking to a freely available work that was posted **without** the rightholder's consent is not a communication to the public if the linker acts without financial gain and does not know, and cannot reasonably know, that the posting was unlawful. Where the link is posted **for profit**, the linker is presumed to know. — [Jones Day, GS Media](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2016/09/the-cjeus-decision-in-igs-mediai-connecting-the-dots-on-hyperlinking); [European Papers, GS Media](https://www.europeanpapers.eu/europeanforum/copyright-protection-digital-era-hyperlinking-and-right-communication)
- **VG Bild-Kunst v Stiftung Preußischer Kulturbesitz (C-392/19, Grand Chamber, 9 March 2021, ECLI:EU:C:2021:181):** "the embedding by means of framing, in a website [...] is a communication to the public where it circumvents protection measures against framing adopted or imposed by the copyright holder." — [CURIA press-release page via search](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/p1_3453439); [FRA case-law reference, C-392/19](https://fra.europa.eu/en/caselaw-reference/cjeu-case-c-39219-judgment)
- The Court also held that a rightholder **cannot restrict consent to framing in any way other than through effective technological measures**. Without such measures, "it might prove difficult, particularly for individual users, to ascertain whether that right holder intended to oppose the framing". — [DLA Piper, "VG Bild-Kunst: the saga on legality of linking continues"](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2021/03/vg-bild-kunst-the-saga-on-legality-of-linking-continues); [Lexology summary](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=c3e2c2d7-8752-4358-bcfa-eb937c6a85bb); [Latham & Watkins, practical implications](https://www.latham.london/2021/04/vg-bild-kunst-practical-implications-of-the-cjeus-latest-decision-on-linking-and-framing/)
- One summary puts the practical consequence this way: circumventing technological restrictions, or ignoring a contractual requirement to apply them, can infringe the communication-to-the-public right. — [Taylor Wessing, "Communicating to the public after VG Bild-Kunst and TuneIn"](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/interface/2021/copyright-update/communicating-to-the-public-after-vg-bild-kunst-and-tunein)
- Background facts: the dispute concerned the Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek (DDB), run by SPK. VG Bild-Kunst would license its repertoire only if DDB used technical measures against third parties framing its thumbnails. In the proceedings, **VG Bild-Kunst argued that there were two simple options for technical protection against framing: a "framekiller" or the "X-frame option"**. This was a litigant's argument, not a holding of the Court. — [CMS AI & copyright case tracker, DDB v VG Bild-Kunst](https://cms.law/en/bel/publication/artificial-intelligence-and-copyright-case-tracker/germany-ddb-v-v.-bild-kunst)
- Aftermath: in **March 2024** the parties settled. DDB does not have to take technical measures against third-party framing of images on its portals, so no German national judgment applied the CJEU test to the facts. — [CMS case tracker](https://cms.law/en/bel/publication/artificial-intelligence-and-copyright-case-tracker/germany-ddb-v-v.-bild-kunst)
- **AG Szpunar's Opinion (Sept 2020)** distinguished clickable links from automatic or embedded links. He reasoned that anti-framing measures "restrict neither access to a work nor even a means of accessing it, but only a manner of displaying works", and so would not qualify for Art. 6 TPM legal protection. Other summaries describe his view of measures against embedded non-clickable links differently. The Court did not adopt his overall approach. — [CMS case tracker](https://cms.law/en/bel/publication/artificial-intelligence-and-copyright-case-tracker/germany-ddb-v-v.-bild-kunst); [Kluwer Copyright Blog, AG Opinion Part I](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/copyright-law-trapped-in-the-web-of-hyperlinking-the-ags-opinion-in-the-vg-bild-kunst-case-part-i/)
- Critiques: commentators say the judgment leaves open the risk that anti-framing measures lock up public-domain works or override exceptions, and that technical protection "may not be possible in every (or even the majority of) cases". — [Lexology / DLA Piper summaries](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=a61ae1a4-702f-461f-9567-fc701886c8ec); [de Bandt](https://www.debandt.eu/en/node/451)
- IPKat (Eleonora Rosati, 9 Mar 2021) titled its post roughly "CJEU rules that linking can be restricted by contract, though only by using effective technological measures". Kluwer Copyright Blog covered the judgment on 15 Mar 2021. Both are cited by MediaLaws, but neither post was read directly. — [MediaLaws article (title/abstract via search)](https://www.medialaws.eu/the-cjeus-take-on-unauthorized-framing-of-online-content-only-if-technologically-precluded-then-prohibited/)
- **Technical facts:** X-Frame-Options (DENY / SAMEORIGIN) tells the browser whether it may render a page in a frame or iframe. CSP `frame-ancestors` overrides XFO when both are present. Browser extensions exist whose sole purpose is to strip these headers, for example the Firefox add-on "Ignore X-Frame-Options", which "allows all sites to be loaded in iframes, despite X-Frame-Options header settings". — [GitHub: Moz-Ext-Ignore-X-Frame-Options](https://github.com/ThomazPom/Moz-Ext-Ignore-X-Frame-Options); [iframetest.com, XFO/CSP bypass explainer](https://iframetest.com/x-frame-options-bypass/); [Usama Ejaz, bypassing XFO/CSP in a WebExtension](https://usamaejaz.com/bypassing-security-iframe-webextension); [Wikipedia: Framekiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framekiller)

### Inferences
- **Operative wording [background knowledge, unverified this session; check on curia/EUR-Lex]:** the ruling reads, in substance, that Art. 3(1) InfoSoc "must be interpreted as meaning that the embedding in a website of a third party, by means of the technique of framing, of works protected by copyright and that are freely accessible to the public with the authorisation of the copyright holder on another website, where that embedding circumvents measures adopted or imposed by that copyright holder to protect against framing, constitutes a communication to the public", and that the rightholder "may not limit his or her consent by means other than effective technological measures, within the meaning of Article 6(1) and (3)" of the Directive. The cross-reference to Art. 6(3) suggests that "effective" is to be read using the TPM definition.
- **Does VG Bild-Kunst cover X-Frame-Options/CSP?** Probably yes in principle, though it is untested.
  - XFO/CSP is the standard, browser-enforced way to oppose framing.
  - The claimant in the case named it as an example of the kind of measure at issue.
  - Every major browser honours it by default, so ordinary users cannot frame the page without deliberately installing circumvention tooling. That arguably meets the Art. 6(3) idea of a measure that "achieves the protection objective".
  - Counter-arguments:
    - (i) Many sites send XFO/CSP for **security** reasons (anti-clickjacking), or because frameworks and CDNs set them by default. The rightholder's intention to restrict *copyright* consent is therefore weaker than with a deliberate, licence-driven measure. Note, though, that the Court's test looks at objective technical measures precisely so that users need not guess intent.
    - (ii) The header is trivially strippable on the client side. However, Art. 6 TPM case law (Nintendo, see section 2) reads "effective" broadly and does not require the measure to be unbreakable.
  - For a framing site that deliberately defeats the header, the "no technical means of opposing" justification in Svensson and BestWater falls away. That is exactly the scenario VG Bild-Kunst addresses.
- **Without circumvention** (games that send no XFO/CSP and are freely accessible): under Svensson and BestWater, iframing them is *not* a communication to the public under EU copyright law, even for profit. GS Media's profit presumption only matters where the framed content was itself posted without consent, for example framing an infringing Wordle clone.
- **With circumvention via an extension**, the "new public" theory works like this: the rightholder's targeted public is only users who visit the original site directly. Framing the page into a different site after defeating the restriction reaches a public the rightholder did not consider. That is the VG Bild-Kunst reasoning.
- The Court treated framing as using the "same technical means" (the internet) as the original posting, so liability turns entirely on the new-public test. Framing a whole game page is closer to BestWater (whole embedded video or work) than to thumbnails, so nothing in that distinction helps the framer.

### Gaps
- Primary texts could not be read: the judgment paragraphs (especially around paras 37–55 on new public and effective measures) and the operative part need direct verification on curia.europa.eu or EUR-Lex.
- No CJEU, national EU or EEA court decision was found that applies VG Bild-Kunst specifically to X-Frame-Options or CSP `frame-ancestors`, or to header-stripping extensions. This is an **open question**.
- No source was found on whether a security-motivated or default-configured XFO header (as opposed to a deliberate copyright measure) satisfies the "adopted or imposed by the copyright holder" element.
- The later CJEU communication-to-the-public cases (e.g. Renckhoff C-161/17 on re-posting copies, Ziggo/Pirate Bay C-610/15 on the "indispensable role" of intermediaries) were not researched this session. They are relevant background **[background knowledge]**.

## 2. Is circumvention a "new public" communication? Does InfoSoc art. 6 (TPM) apply to a header-stripping extension?

### Takeaway
There are two separate legal hooks:
1. **Communication to the public (Art. 3).** Under VG Bild-Kunst, framing that circumvents anti-framing measures is a communication to a *new public* and needs authorisation. This is the more solid hook.
2. **Anti-circumvention (Art. 6).** This hook is **unsettled** but plausible. Art. 6(1) prohibits circumventing "effective technological measures". Art. 6(2) prohibits distributing devices or services primarily designed to circumvent them. Nintendo (C-355/12) reads "effective technological measure" broadly. AG Szpunar thought anti-framing measures would not qualify, because they control only the manner of display and not access. The Court's cross-reference to Art. 6(1) and (3) points the other way.

### Cited Findings
- VG Bild-Kunst: circumventing the rightholder's anti-framing measures turns framing into a communication to the public that needs authorisation. — [CURIA](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/p1_3453439); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/interface/2021/copyright-update/communicating-to-the-public-after-vg-bild-kunst-and-tunein)
- AG Szpunar: anti-framing measures restrict "only a manner of displaying works" and are "not therefore eligible for the legal protection provided for by the directive" (Art. 6). — [CMS case tracker](https://cms.law/en/bel/publication/artificial-intelligence-and-copyright-case-tracker/germany-ddb-v-v.-bild-kunst). Another summary describes the AG as treating measures against embedded non-clickable links as "effective protection measures" within Art. 6. The two sources characterise him differently and should be checked against the Opinion. — [Lexology summary](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=a61ae1a4-702f-461f-9567-fc701886c8ec)
- **Nintendo v PC Box (C-355/12, 23 Jan 2014):**
  - "effective technological measure" is to be understood broadly. It covers access codes or protection processes such as encryption or scrambling.
  - Measures can sit in devices, not only in the works themselves.
  - For Art. 6(2), it is not necessary to consider the particular intended use the rightholder attributes to a device.
  - The protection is subject to **proportionality**: legal protection only covers measures that pursue the objective of preventing unauthorised acts, and it must not prohibit devices with significant other legitimate uses.
  — [SCL, ECJ judgment on circumvention of games console protection](https://www.scl.org/3002-ecj-judgment-on-circumvention-of-games-console-protection/); [IPKat, "CJEU says that circumventing a protection system may be lawful (sometimes)"](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2014/01/breaking-news-cjeu-says-that.html); [SCL, "Game Over for Excessive TPM?"](https://www.scl.org/3014-game-over-for-excessive-tpm-the-nintendo-ruling/); [EU Law Radar](https://eulawradar.com/case-c-35512-nintendo-a-proportionate-response-to-game-piracy-or-an-unjust-market-foreclosure/)
- Art. 6(2) targets devices "primarily designed, produced, adapted or performed for the purpose of enabling or facilitating the circumvention" of effective measures. Art. 6(1) covers circumvention carried out "in the knowledge, or with reasonable grounds to know" that one is pursuing that objective. — [SCL](https://www.scl.org/3002-ecj-judgment-on-circumvention-of-games-console-protection/); [Springer IIC article on Nintendo](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40319-014-0223-x)
- Header-stripping extensions are marketed for exactly this purpose: "Allows all sites to be loaded in iframes, despite X-Frame-Options header settings". — [GitHub: Moz-Ext-Ignore-X-Frame-Options](https://github.com/ThomazPom/Moz-Ext-Ignore-X-Frame-Options)

### Inferences
- **Art. 6(3) definition [background knowledge, unverified this session]:**
  - "Technological measures" means any technology, device or component that, in the normal course of its operation, is designed to prevent or restrict acts, in respect of works, that the rightholder has not authorised.
  - Such measures are "effective" where use is controlled through an access control or protection process that achieves the protection objective.
  - An XFO/CSP header is designed to prevent framing. After VG Bild-Kunst, framing in that situation is an act requiring authorisation (communication to the public), so the header plausibly "restricts acts not authorised by the rightholder".
  - That makes the AG's "only manner of display" objection weaker after the judgment. Because framing in those circumstances *is* a restricted act, a measure blocking it arguably restricts a restricted act.
- **Art. 6(2) device-distribution exposure (the main risk for the person distributing the extension):** an extension whose primary or sole purpose is stripping XFO/CSP so that blocked sites can be framed fits the language "primarily designed [...] for the purpose of enabling or facilitating the circumvention" reasonably well.
  - A general developer tool with significant other uses would have a better proportionality argument under Nintendo. Examples are debugging, testing your own sites, or accessibility.
  - A purpose-built "make the game feed work with NYT Wordle" extension would not.
- **Practical point:** VG Bild-Kunst makes the TPM question partly academic for the site operator. Even if XFO/CSP is not an Art. 6 TPM, framing after circumventing it can still be a primary infringement of the communication right under Art. 3.
- The **legitimate games that send no anti-framing headers** are a different case. Framing them is generally lawful in the EU under BestWater, subject to non-copyright issues such as trademarks, terms of use, unfair competition and passing off.

### Gaps
- No court decision was found, in the EU, EEA or US, holding that X-Frame-Options or CSP `frame-ancestors` is, or is not, an "effective technological measure" under Art. 6 InfoSoc, Norwegian åndsverkloven § 99, or US DMCA § 1201. **Open question.**
- US DMCA § 1201 was not researched in depth. Whether XFO "effectively controls access" (§ 1201(a)), which is doubtful because the page itself remains directly accessible, or "protects a right" (§ 1201(b), e.g. the display right) is unverified. No case was found.

## 3. Norway/EEA: åndsverkloven 2018, TPM provisions, EEA reception of CJEU case law, Norwegian decisions on linking/framing

### Takeaway
Norway implements the InfoSoc Directive through the EEA Agreement. The 2018 Copyright Act (åndsverkloven, lov 15. juni 2018 nr. 40) gives the author the exclusive right to make the work available to the public (§ 2) and prohibits circumventing "effective technical protection systems" (§ 99). Under the EEA homogeneity principle, Norwegian courts treat CJEU case law such as Svensson, BestWater, GS Media and VG Bild-Kunst as highly relevant, even though Norway is not formally bound the way EU states are. The only Norwegian Supreme Court decision found on linking is **Napster.no (Rt. 2005 s. 41)**. It imposed **contributory liability (medvirkning)** for linking to unlawfully uploaded MP3 files and did not decide whether linking itself is "making available". No Norwegian decision on framing, on VG Bild-Kunst, or on header circumvention was found.

### Cited Findings
- Åndsverkloven 2018 **§ 99** ("Forbud mot omgåelse mv. av effektive tekniske beskyttelsessystemer"): "Det er forbudt å omgå effektive tekniske beskyttelsessystemer som en rettighetshaver eller den rettighetshaveren har gitt samtykke, benytter for å kontrollere eksemplarfremstilling eller tilgjengeliggjøring for allmennheten av et vernet verk." In English: it is forbidden to circumvent effective technical protection systems that a rightholder, or someone the rightholder has consented to, uses to control reproduction or making available to the public of a protected work. — [Lovdata, åndsverkloven kap. 7 § 99 (via search snippet)](https://lovdata.no/nav/lov/2018-06-15-40/kap7/%C2%A7101); [Stortinget lovvedtak 53 (2017–2018)](https://www.stortinget.no/no/Saker-og-publikasjoner/Vedtak/Beslutninger/Lovvedtak/2017-2018/vedtak-201718-053/)
- The 2018 Act replaced the 1961 Act. The former circumvention provision was § 53a, introduced by amendment act 17 June 2005 no. 97, which implemented the InfoSoc Directive. — [Regjeringen høringsnotat, ny åndsverklov](https://www.regjeringen.no/contentassets/4c75d8945bc44691925253ca9d0971b5/hoeringsnotat_andsverklov.pdf); [Lovdata, endringslov 2005-06-17-97](https://lovdata.no/dokument/LTI/lov/2005-06-17-97)
- **Napster.no, Rt. 2005 s. 41 (HR-2005-00133-A):**
  - The Supreme Court held the site operator liable for **contributory infringement (medvirkning)** to the unlawful making-available of music.
  - The linked files had been uploaded without consent by others, not by Napster.no.
  - The Court **did not decide** whether linking itself amounted to making the files available to the public.
  — [Jusnytt, "Napster – ansvar for tilgjengeliggjøring"](https://www.jusnytt.no/en/de-siste-nyhetene/1-napster-ansvar-for-tilgjengeliggjoring); [Supreme Court judgment PDF (domstol.no, not reachable this session)](https://www.domstol.no/globalassets/upload/hret/avgjorelser/2005/januar/hr-2005-00133-a.pdf)
- The government's consultation paper for the 2018 Act notes that the Napster decision has been criticised, that legal commentary is divided on linking liability, and that linking may appear at first sight to be a form of making available. — [Regjeringen høringsnotat](https://www.regjeringen.no/contentassets/4c75d8945bc44691925253ca9d0971b5/hoeringsnotat_andsverklov.pdf)
- A secondary source describes the Norwegian Supreme Court in Napster as having rejected treating hyperlinking itself as a qualifying act, which it says aligns with the AG's reasoning in GS Media. This characterisation is somewhat stronger than Jusnytt's ("left open"). — [KCL Student Law Review, "Hyperlinking and Copyright Infringement in the EU"](https://blogs.kcl.ac.uk/kslr/files/2020/05/a-4-x-ii_final-compressed.pdf); compare [Jusnytt](https://www.jusnytt.no/en/de-siste-nyhetene/1-napster-ansvar-for-tilgjengeliggjoring)
- A UiB master's thesis on whether external linking can be independent making-available reviews Norwegian, Swedish and Danish case law. It finds fundamental differences in reasoning between the three countries. — [BORA UiB thesis](https://bora.uib.no/bora-xmlui/bitstream/1956/3616/1/58409120.pdf)
- A Swedish commentary on VG Bild-Kunst by Johan Axhamn (Lund University) exists. It is relevant to Nordic reception but could not be read. — [Axhamn, Kommentar C-392/19 (PDF, not reachable)](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/96421284/Axhamn_Kommentar_m_l_C_392_19_VG_Bild_Kunst_210407b.pdf)

### Inferences
- **§ 2 [background knowledge, unverified this session]:** § 2 of the 2018 Act gives the author the exclusive right to produce copies and to make the work available to the public. "Tilgjengeliggjøring for allmennheten" includes "overføring til allmennheten" (communication to the public, including on-demand making available), mirroring InfoSoc Art. 3. Norwegian courts are expected to interpret it consistently with CJEU Art. 3 case law.
- **EEA reception [background knowledge, unverified this session]:**
  - The InfoSoc Directive (2001/29/EC) is incorporated into the EEA Agreement (Annex XVII).
  - EEA Art. 6 requires conformity with CJEU rulings given before the Agreement was signed (2 May 1992).
  - For later rulings, the EFTA Court must pay "due account" to them (Surveillance and Court Agreement, Art. 3(2)). In practice the EFTA Court and the Norwegian Supreme Court follow them to secure homogeneity.
  - CJEU judgments are therefore not formally binding in Norway, but they are a heavy source of law (tung rettskildefaktor). A Norwegian court would very likely apply Svensson, BestWater and VG Bild-Kunst to framing.
  - The search result confirms only the general point that "EØS-avtalens homogenitetsprinsipp gjør at EU-domstolens praksis er en viktig tolkningsfaktor". That statement is from a search summary, not a cited authority.
- **§ 99 applied to header stripping:** the Norwegian wording "kontrollere [...] tilgjengeliggjøring for allmennheten" (control making available to the public) fits a measure that controls framing. After VG Bild-Kunst, framing in those circumstances is a making-available act. So stripping XFO/CSP could plausibly fall under § 99 on the same untested reasoning as Art. 6 InfoSoc.
  - The device ban (InfoSoc Art. 6(2), implemented in Norway, likely in § 99 second paragraph or an adjacent section) would be the main exposure for distributing the extension.
  - **[Background knowledge, unverified]:** Norwegian law historically included a specific carve-out that circumvention for private use of lawfully acquired copies (e.g. playing a DVD) is not punishable. Its scope in the 2018 Act needs verification on Lovdata.
- **Napster.no shows Norwegian courts readily use contributory liability (medvirkning) for people who facilitate others' infringing communication.** This is relevant to an operator who distributes or instructs users to install a header-stripping extension. Even if the operator's own framing were not a primary act, contributory liability is a realistic alternative.
- **Remedies [background knowledge, unverified]:** the 2018 Act has provisions on criminal penalties, compensation and remuneration (vederlag/erstatning, in chapter 8 around §§ 79–83). The exact section numbers were not verified.

### Gaps
- No Norwegian court decision (Supreme Court, appellate or district) on **framing**, on applying **Svensson/GS Media/VG Bild-Kunst**, or on circumventing anti-framing headers was found. Lovdata and domstol.no could not be reached.
- The exact wording of åndsverkloven § 2, the full text of § 99 (including the device-distribution paragraph and any private-use carve-out), and the penalty and compensation sections were not verified directly.
- Norway's implementation status of the 2019 DSM Directive (2019/790) was not verified. A 2025–2026 government bill (Prop. … L (2025–2026), regjeringen.no document "prp202520260041") appeared in search results but was not read. It is probably not central to framing, but it may matter for Art. 15 press publishers' rights or Art. 17 platform rules.

## 4. US comparison: Perfect 10 server test and the split (Goldman, Nicklen, McGucken, Hunley)

### Takeaway
In the **Ninth Circuit** (California and other western states), the "server test" from *Perfect 10 v. Amazon* (2007) remains binding: a site that embeds content hosted on someone else's server does not "display" it and is not a direct infringer. *Hunley v. Instagram* (2023) reaffirmed the test, and the Supreme Court declined to review *McGucken v. Valnet* in 2025. District courts in the **Second Circuit** (New York) have rejected the test: *Goldman v. Breitbart* (2018), *Nicklen v. Sinclair* (2021), *McGucken v. Newsweek* (2022). The Second Circuit itself sidestepped the question in April 2026 and decided its case on licence grounds. US law has no VG Bild-Kunst-style rule tied to anti-framing measures.

### Cited Findings
- **Hunley v. Instagram, LLC, 73 F.4th 1060 (9th Cir. July 17, 2023):** affirmed that the server test applies to embedded content. Embedding sites that do not store copies cannot be directly liable for display. The court refused to limit the test to search engines and rejected the argument that *Aereo* undermined *Perfect 10*. — [Justia, 9th Cir. opinion No. 22-15293](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/22-15293/22-15293-2023-07-17.html); [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/insights/2023/08/server-test-instagram-case-ninth-circuit); [Technology & Marketing Law Blog (Goldman) guest post](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2023/08/ninth-circuit-reaffirms-the-server-test-for-direct-infringement-of-the-public-display-right-hunley-v-instagram-llc-guest-blog-post.htm); [Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/blogs/incontestable/embedding-and-the-server-test-survive-in-the-ninth-circuitfor-now.html)
- **Goldman v. Breitbart News (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 2018, Judge Forest):** embedding a tweet containing a copyrighted photo can violate the display right. The court declined to apply the server test and granted partial summary judgment to Goldman. It was certified for interlocutory appeal in March 2018. The appeal's outcome was not found; it is reportedly resolved without a Second Circuit merits ruling **[unverified]**. — [Foley & Lardner](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2018/03/case-to-watch-goldman-v-breitbart-involving-embedd/); [Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/zh-hans/insights/publications/2018/02/goldman-v-breitbart-news-network-llc); [CDT](https://cdt.org/?p=13410)
- **Nicklen v. Sinclair Broadcast (S.D.N.Y. 2021, Judge Rakoff):** embedding a video "displays" it. The server test is contrary to the text and legislative history of the Copyright Act. — [Weil alert](https://www.weil.com/-/media/mailings/2021/q2/ip-media-alert-august-9-2021embedding-the-server-test-rift-sdny-decision-bucks-ninth-circuit-once-ag.pdf); [Proskauer New Media & Technology Law blog](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/tag/server-test/)
- **McGucken v. Newsweek (S.D.N.Y. March 21, 2022):** followed Nicklen. The court observed that "the server test has not been widely adopted outside of the Ninth Circuit", held that Newsweek displayed the photo, and left fair use for trial. It never reached a definitive merits ruling or appeal. — [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/publications/2022/03/another-blow-to-ninth-circuits-server-test); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/sdny-again-rejects-server-test-display-embedded-content); [Technology & Marketing Law Blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/?p=23772)
- **McGucken v. Valnet (9th Cir.; cert. petition No. 24-1040):** the district court dismissed under the server test and the Ninth Circuit affirmed. The **Supreme Court denied certiorari in 2025**; the Nixon Peabody alert is dated 9 July 2025. — [Nixon Peabody](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2025/07/09/supreme-court-declines-to-resolve-looming-split-on-embedded-content-rights); [Supreme Court docket petition PDF](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/24/24-1040/354280/20250328145643075_24-%20Petition.pdf); [Crowell](https://crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/will-the-supreme-court-address-whether-the-ninth-circuits-server-test-comports-with-the-display-right-accorded-copyright-owners)
- **Second Circuit (April 23, 2026), Richardson v. Townsquare Media:**
  - The court said it had "no occasion to resolve" the server test.
  - It held that YouTube's terms "unambiguously" granted a licence covering Townsquare's embedding.
  - As a result, district courts in the Second Circuit continue to decline the server test, while it remains binding in the Ninth Circuit.
  — [Proskauer blog (via search summary)](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/second-circuit-sidesteps-server-test-in-embedded-video-copyright-ruling-highlighting-how-platform-terms-matter)
- NYT has used DMCA takedowns against Wordle clones (2022 and March 2024, roughly 1,900 GitHub forks targeted). — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/puzzle/nearly-2000-wordle-clones-targeted-as-nyt-issues-dmca-takedown/); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/the-new-york-times-is-cracking-down-on-wordle-clones-100004668.html)

### Inferences
- **Iframes in the US:** an iframe loads content directly from the game owner's server, which is exactly the server-test fact pattern. Under Ninth Circuit law, the framing site is likely not a *direct* infringer of the display right. Secondary liability, DMCA § 1201, contract (terms of use), trademark and unfair competition remain separate theories.
- **Elsewhere in the US**, a New York court could find that iframing a game page "displays" it, a public display or performance by the framing site. The framer would then rely on fair use, which is weak for wholesale framing of a complete game for a competing feed, or on an implied licence, which is undermined where the owner actively sends anti-framing headers.
- **Relevance to a Norway-based operator:** US courts can assert jurisdiction over foreign sites that target US users, and US rightholders (e.g. NYT) can send DMCA notices to US-based hosts, CDNs, app or extension stores and GitHub regardless of the operator's location. So the US split matters practically even if Norwegian law governs the operator's primary liability. **[Inference; jurisdiction not researched.]**
- Actively stripping a rightholder's anti-framing headers would undercut any implied-licence or good-faith argument in the US, and could invite a § 1201 claim. That claim is untested and doubtful as an "access control", but it is not frivolous.

### Gaps
- The outcome of the Goldman v. Breitbart interlocutory appeal, and the exact date of the McGucken v. Valnet cert denial, were not verified.
- No US case was found on header-stripping or anti-framing circumvention under § 1201.
- Personal jurisdiction and choice of law over a Norway-based site accessible in the US were not researched.

## 5. Are game pages copyrightable works? Game vs. expression

### Takeaway
The **code** (as a computer program and literary work), **graphics, artwork, sounds and original text** of a web game are copyrightable. **Game mechanics, rules and ideas are not.** Elements dictated by function, such as Wordle's grid and colour feedback, are contested. The NYT claims them as protected, while academics doubt it. Curated word or answer lists may be protected, but this is unsettled and no case law was found. Under EU law, a GUI is not protected *as a computer program* (BSA C-393/09), but it can be protected as an ordinary work if original. For framing this matters little: an iframe communicates the entire rendered page, which contains protected expression (code, art, text), whatever the status of the underlying game idea.

### Cited Findings
- Copyright does not protect game mechanics or rules, only their particular expression. "You cannot copyright an idea, so the game mechanics could not be protected." — [Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2022/01/12/world-handled-wordle-ripoff-just-fine-without-any-ip-action/); [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/wordle-clones-show-difficulties-of-protecting-apps-from-greedy-copycats)
- NYT's takedown notices claimed the 5x6 grid, green and yellow tiles, and the keyboard beneath the grid. An IP law professor quoted by AP said the grid is not copyrightable because it is dictated by the game's rules. NYT says it has no issue with similar games that do not infringe its Wordle "trademarks or copyrighted gameplay". — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/the-new-york-times-is-cracking-down-on-wordle-clones-100004668.html); [Buffalo News / AP](https://buffalonews.com/life-entertainment/new-york-times-wordle-clones-takedown-dmca/article_403324df-89ee-51a3-b625-6b8beb59f736.html); [Copyright Lately, "War of the Wordles"](https://copyrightlately.com/war-of-the-wordles-did-the-new-york-times-go-too-far/)
- Word lists: the only source found is a discussion-forum opinion that exact copying of the list is probably infringing. It is not authoritative. — [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=39645991)
- **BSA (C-393/09, 22 Dec 2010):** a GUI is not a form of expression of a computer program under the Software Directive, so it is not protected as a program. — [Kluwer Copyright Blog on BSA](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/protection-of-guis-graphical-user-interfaces-some-comments-about-the-ecj-s-preliminary-ruling-in-bsa-v-ministervo-kultury/)
- **SAS Institute (C-406/10, 2 May 2012):** a program's functionality, programming language and data file formats are not protected, only the expression. — [UK government copy of SAS judgment](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/698582/Case-C-406-10-SAS-Institute.pdf); [Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://copyrightblog.kluweriplaw.com/2012/05/07/decrypting-the-code-cjeu-sas-vs-world-programming/?output=pdf)

### Inferences
- **[Background knowledge, unverified]:**
  - BSA also held that a GUI *can* be protected as a work under the InfoSoc Directive if it is its author's own intellectual creation.
  - US case law such as *Tetris Holding v. Xio* (D.N.J. 2012) shows that a game's overall visual expression can be protected even though the mechanics are not.
  - Norwegian law protects computer programs and literary or artistic works under åndsverkloven § 2 (list of protected subject matter).
- For iframing, the question is whether the framed page **as communicated** contains protected works, not whether the "game" is protected. A complete web game page (JavaScript source, art, logo, text and puzzles) almost certainly does. So the VG Bild-Kunst analysis applies.
- Small indie daily games (Worldle, Globle, Flagle, etc.) carry the same copyright in code, graphics and maps or flags. Note that map and flag data may be public domain or under third-party licences.

### Gaps
- No court decision on the copyrightability of Wordle-style word lists, or on Wordle trade dress, was found. Trademark aspects (NYT owns the "Wordle" mark) and passing off were not researched.

## 6. Who bears liability: the site operator, the extension distributor, or the end user?

### Takeaway
The framing site operator is the most exposed party. Under VG Bild-Kunst it is the one making the communication to the public when its framing circumvents anti-framing measures. If an extension is needed, the operator's role in designing the feed around circumvention, and in distributing or instructing installation of the extension, strengthens that conclusion. It also opens contributory liability (Napster.no). An extension distributor faces device-trafficking exposure under InfoSoc Art. 6(2) and åndsverkloven § 99. That exposure is strongest for a purpose-built extension, while general developer tools have proportionality defences (Nintendo). End users who install the extension perform the circumvention act themselves. Their exposure is theoretically under Art. 6(1)/§ 99, but enforcement against individuals is unlikely. None of this allocation has been tested in court for anti-framing headers.

### Cited Findings
- VG Bild-Kunst places the communication-to-the-public act on the party that embeds by framing while circumventing protection measures. — [CURIA](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/p1_3453439); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2021/03/vg-bild-kunst-the-saga-on-legality-of-linking-continues)
- Napster.no (Rt. 2005 s. 41): the Norwegian Supreme Court imposed contributory (medvirkning) liability on a site that linked to unlawfully uploaded files, without needing to decide whether linking itself was making available. — [Jusnytt](https://www.jusnytt.no/en/de-siste-nyhetene/1-napster-ansvar-for-tilgjengeliggjoring)
- Art. 6(2) InfoSoc targets the supply of devices "primarily designed [...] for the purpose of enabling or facilitating the circumvention". Art. 6(1) targets circumvention with knowledge or reasonable grounds to know. Under Nintendo, protection must be proportionate and the devices' other uses are relevant. — [SCL](https://www.scl.org/3002-ecj-judgment-on-circumvention-of-games-console-protection/); [IPKat](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2014/01/breaking-news-cjeu-says-that.html)
- Åndsverkloven § 99 prohibits circumventing effective technical protection systems used to control making available to the public. — [Lovdata (search snippet)](https://lovdata.no/nav/lov/2018-06-15-40/kap7/%C2%A7101)
- Header-stripping operates in the user's own browser, so it "only affects your own browser". — [iframetest.com](https://iframetest.com/x-frame-options-bypass/)
- US: under the server test, embedding sites that do not host copies are not direct infringers in the Ninth Circuit, but New York district courts disagree. — [Justia, Hunley](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/22-15293/22-15293-2023-07-17.html); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/sdny-again-rejects-server-test-display-embedded-content)

### Inferences
- **Site operator:**
  - The "header-stripping happens in the user's browser" argument (the operator never circumvents anything itself) is unlikely to help where the operator builds a public feed that depends on the extension and tells users to install it.
  - CJEU case law looks at who plays an "indispensable role" in giving the public access with full knowledge of the consequences **[background knowledge: GS Media; Stichting Brein v Filmspeler C-527/15; Ziggo C-610/15]**. That points to the operator.
  - At minimum, Norwegian medvirkning (Napster.no) supplies a route to liability.
- **Lower-risk configuration:** frame only games that send no XFO/CSP or that explicitly allow framing. That fits the Svensson and BestWater safe zone. Show a link or card (open in new tab) for blocked games instead of framing them. A plain hyperlink to a freely accessible page is lawful under Svensson.
  - Getting permission or a licence (some indie developers may welcome it) removes the copyright issue entirely.
  - These are inferences from the case law, not legal advice.
- **Extension distributor:** the operator and the distributor may be the same person, which compounds exposure. Distribution through the Chrome Web Store or Firefox Add-ons also brings platform-policy and DMCA-notice risk in the US.
- **End user:** theoretically within Art. 6(1)/§ 99 if they knowingly circumvent. Individual enforcement is improbable, and any private-use carve-out in Norwegian law needs verification.
- **Non-copyright risks** are outside scope but relevant: terms of service of game sites (NYT's terms may prohibit framing; not verified), trademark and passing off (the "Wordle" mark), unfair competition / markedsføringsloven, security implications of disabling clickjacking protection for users, and ad-revenue displacement arguments that strengthen a rightholder's motivation to sue.

### Gaps
- No case was found allocating liability for anti-framing circumvention among operator, extension developer and user, in any jurisdiction.
- The NYT Games terms of service text on framing was not verified.
- Whether Norwegian law (or EU law, via the private-copying framework) exempts end-user circumvention for private, non-commercial viewing was not verified.
